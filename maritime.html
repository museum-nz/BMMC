The Mosquito Fleet, Dangerous Reefs & Sentinels of Light

Before railways, New Zealand’s rugged coastline was its true highway. Shallow-draught scows, cutters, and steamers—fondly named the Mosquito Fleet—ferried timber, butter, wool, and pioneers across bar harbours, while hazardous rocks and wild Southern Ocean swells gave birth to tragic shipwrecks and a nationwide chain of coastal lighthouses.

Wreck & Salvage Lore: Disasters and Miraculous Rescues

Historic tragedies that reformed navigation safety alongside legendary coastal groundings

131 Lives Lost • 1881 Waipapa Point

The Catlins Tragedy & The Birth of Waipapa Lighthouse

Struck Otara Reef on 29 April 1881. The 20-hour ordeal claimed 131 lives and led directly to the building of the Waipapa Point Lighthouse.

En route from Port Chalmers to Melbourne via Bluff, the passenger steamer struck Otara Reef. 131 passengers and crew drowned while onshore onlookers stood helplessly. Victims rest in the historic Tararua Acre in Fortrose.

Heritage Impact: Most victims were buried in the historic Tararua Acre cemetery nearby in Fortrose.

53 Lives Lost • 1968 Barrett Reef

Cyclone Giselle & The TEV Wahine Disaster

Twin-screw passenger-rail ferry struck Barrett Reef in hurricane-force winds during Cyclone Giselle on 10 April 1968; New Zealand's worst modern maritime disaster.

Crossing from Lyttelton with 734 passengers and crew, 100-knot winds blew the vessel onto Barrett Reef, ripping off her starboard propeller. She foundered off Steeple Rock, claiming 53 lives in heavy surf.

Memorial: Wahine Memorial Park in Seatoun displays the ship’s foremast and anchor overlooking the harbour entrance.

0 Casualties • 1874 Surat Bay / Catlins

The Full-Rigged Surat: 271 Immigrants Safely Landed

Full-rigged immigrant clipper struck a reef; the captain attempted to sail on before beaching at Surat Bay, where all 271 immigrants were safely landed without a single loss of life.

Carrying immigrant families to Dunedin, the Surat struck reef rock. Despite rising water, the captain kept sailing before beaching on the sand at False Islet. Local settlers sheltered all 271 souls without injury.

Legacy: Surat Bay in the Catlins was permanently renamed after the vessel and is now a tranquil sea lion sanctuary.

Early Southern Whaling & Sealing Shore Stations (1822–1850s)

The first European-Māori frontier communities in Murihiku / Southland

Est. 1829 • Cuttle Cove Fiordland

Preservation Inlet (Rakituma)

Established by Australian trader Peter Williams at Cuttle Cove in 1829, this was mainland Southland's first commercial shore whaling station. Try-pots boiled down right whale blubber into valuable train oil. Later in the 1890s, the nearby gold mining towns of Cromarty and Te Oneroa bloomed, guarded by the isolated Puysegur Point Light.

Fiordland Wilderness
Est. 1836 • Motupōhue Bluff Port

Stirling Point & Bluff Harbour

Founded in 1836 beneath Motupōhue; the foundation of Southland's major international deep-water seaport.

Captain William Stirling set up six-oared whaleboats in Foveaux Strait. As whaling declined, Bluff transitioned into the vital export port for southern meat, wool, and the iconic Foveaux Strait oyster fleet.

State Highway 1 Anchor
Est. 1837 • Howell's Point Aparima

Riverton / Aparima & Capt. Howell

Founded in 1837; established the oldest permanent European settlement in Southland in partnership with Ngāi Tahu.

Captain John Howell brought whaleboats from Sydney and married Kohikohi, daughter of chief Horomona Patu, founding a stable, intermarried maritime trading post at Riverton/Aparima.

The Lighthouse Sentinels & Government Life Insurance Stamps

Why New Zealand's coastal beacons became world-famous philatelic icons (1891–1981)

📮 Explore All Lighthouse Stamps in Catalog ↗

When the government abolished free departmental franking privileges in 1890, the Government Life Insurance Department (GLI) chose a coastal lighthouse as its official emblem, symbolizing “security, steadfast guidance through stormy seas, and protection for the home.”

From 1891 until 1981, New Zealand was the only country in the British Empire to issue distinct postage stamps exclusively for its life insurance department, depicting genuine sentinel lights:

  • 💡

    Perched high on the tip of the North Island where the Tasman Sea meets the Pacific Ocean. Last manned light constructed in New Zealand.

    Built in 1941 to replace the inaccessible Motuopao Island light, Cape Reinga's 10-metre white concrete tower marks the northern extremity of New Zealand. Powered by solar-charged batteries with a 19-nautical-mile range.

  • 💡

    Cast-iron tower fabricated in England; Mary Jane Bennett was New Zealand's first and only official female lighthouse keeper.

    Perched high above Wellington Harbour, Pencarrow was cast in Britain. Mary Jane Bennett was formally appointed head keeper from 1859 to 1864.

  • 💡

    Foveaux Strait sentinel guarding Bluff Harbour approach with iconic black and white horizontal bands.

    Designed by James Balfour and constructed of local Bluff stone, Dog Island rises 37 metres, making it New Zealand’s tallest lighthouse tower.

  • 💡

    Cast-iron tower first illuminated on 1 January 1865; New Zealand’s oldest operating lighthouse, now situated in a famous bird sanctuary.

    Fabricated in England and shipped to New Zealand in sections, this 20-metre tower was the most powerful lighthouse in the Southern Hemisphere when fitted with an 11-million candlepower xenon lamp in 1955.

  • 💡

    Constructed directly in response to the SS Tararua disaster; one of the last wooden towers built in NZ.

    Erected in 1884 by the Marine Department, this wooden kauri tower houses a 2nd-order Fresnel lens. It still stands sentinel today on the Southland coast.

  • 💡

    Guarding the dangerous southwestern entrance to Foveaux Strait, notoriously known as the most isolated and wind-swept station in New Zealand.

    Located on the rugged, storm-lashed southwestern tip of the South Island, Puysegur was constructed in 1879. Keepers survived months between supply ship visits.

  • 💡

    Perched on isolated windswept rocks in Cook Strait; very last lighthouse in New Zealand to be automated (1990).

    Built on rocky islets in Cook Strait in 1877. Keepers were hoisted ashore by crane. In 1990 it became the final lighthouse in New Zealand to be demanned.

  • 💡

    Perched on high cliffs in Northland, featured on the 1969 4c Government Life Insurance stamp centenary edition.

    Erected in 1910, Cape Brett warned vessels away from the rocky outer Bay of Islands. Its clockwork mechanism rotated a seven-ton lens floating in a mercury bath.

The Mosquito Fleet: Workhorses of Harbours and Bar Rivers

Flat-bottomed scows, puffing coasters, and the lifeline to frontier towns

Submarines of the Hauraki Gulf & Kauri Trade

In the 1880s to 1920s, hundreds of small steamers, ketches, and distinctive New Zealand scows navigated tidal rivers and shallow sandbars. Built with centreboard drop-keels and shallow flat hulls, scows were celebrated as "the submarines of the coast" because waves often broke straight across their heavily laden flush decks of kauri logs and road shingles.

From Onehunga on the Manukau Harbour, coasters tackled the dangerous Manukau Bar to supply the goldfields of Thames, the coal mines of Westport and Greymouth, and isolated dairy factories throughout Taranaki.

Connecting Farming Outposts to the World

Before the railway reached inland Southland and Otago, farms like Bonniefields depended on local coastal shipping through Bluff and Fortrose. Wool bales, tallow, and butter pats were hauled by dray to estuary jetties and loaded aboard puffing steamers for transfer to ocean-going refrigerated cargo liners.
